What Happens When You Actually Use It

5 min read  ·  Jason Scott, Founder of Plumlo

Most people who try Plumlo don't know exactly what to expect. You paste a job description, Alex introduces herself, and the interview begins. No setup call, no onboarding. It just starts.

Here's what actually happened in two of the first real sessions.

Joanna, sales role

Session 1

Joanna pasted the job description for a sales position she was actively pursuing. Alex pulled the role requirements and opened the interview the same way any recruiter would. A few questions in, the session was moving fast enough that Joanna said it felt real.

After the debrief, she went back to Alex with a question most people don't think to ask. She wanted to know where her gaps were, not just what she'd done well. Alex walked her through a few angles she hadn't addressed in her answers, adjacent competencies the role was likely evaluating that hadn't come up directly in the questions.

That part surprised her. She said it helped greatly. Not just the practice, but the conversation after.

That's something worth sitting with. The debrief is the product. The interview is how you get there.

"She wanted to know where her gaps were. Alex walked her through angles she hadn't addressed, adjacent competencies the role was likely evaluating."

My own session

Session 2

I built Plumlo. I've heard Alex ask questions hundreds of times. I still found it useful to run a session for myself.

After the last question, I asked Alex directly where I had room to improve. She gave me specific feedback on areas where my answers were strong on experience but thin on outcome. I knew what I'd done. I hadn't been clear enough about what it produced.

That's the gap most experienced candidates have. The story is there. The proof isn't.

Both sessions pointed at the same thing. It's not about memorizing answers. It's about understanding how your answers actually land before you're in a room where it counts.

It works for any interview, not just AI ones

Plumlo started as a response to AI recruiting. But what it's actually doing is something older than that. It's giving you honest feedback on how you communicate under pressure, before the stakes are real.

A human interviewer won't tell you your answer was too vague. They won't point out that you talked about the project but never mentioned the result. They'll just move on and you'll never know what they wrote down.

Alex tells you. That's the difference.

  • Going into a panel interview and want to tighten your stories
  • Prepping for a final round and need to know where you're soft
  • Facing an AI screener and have no idea what it's evaluating
  • Just back in the job market after a few years and rusty on interviews

All of it applies. The debrief doesn't know what kind of interview you're preparing for. It just shows you what's working and what isn't.

"A human interviewer won't tell you your answer was too vague. They'll just move on and you'll never know what they wrote down."

What the debrief actually shows you

After every session, Plumlo scores your performance and breaks it into three sections. What landed well, what missed, and what to fix for next time. Each section is specific to the job description you submitted, not generic interview advice.

If the role required someone who could manage cross-functional teams and you never demonstrated that in your answers, the debrief will tell you. If your strongest answer aligned well with a key requirement, you'll know that too. The feedback is built from the role, not a template.

That's what makes it useful for any interview. The criteria change. The format doesn't.
